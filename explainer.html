How you win a percent of fuel efficiency

A commercial turbofan is a chain of compromises tuned toward one number: how far it flies per kilogram of fuel. Here's where that number actually comes from, and every lever a high-efficiency design pulls.

01 The two efficiencies

Overall efficiency — fuel energy in versus propulsive work out — is the product of two independent things. This is the single most useful idea in engine design, because it tells you there are two separate battles:

ηoverall = ηthermal × ηpropulsive

Thermal efficiency is how well the core (compressor → combustor → turbine) converts fuel into mechanical power. Propulsive efficiency is how well that power is converted into actual thrust without throwing energy away in a fast exhaust jet. A brilliant core attached to a bad propulsor is still a bad engine, and vice-versa.

The intuition. Thrust = mass flow × change in velocity. You can get a given thrust by throwing a little air very fast, or a lot of air slightly faster. The wasted energy is the leftover kinetic energy in the exhaust — and that scales with velocity squared. So moving more air, more gently, is fundamentally cheaper. That one fact drives the entire history of the turbofan.

02 Propulsive efficiency: move a lot of air, gently

The bypass ratio (BPR) is the mass of air the fan pushes around the core divided by the mass that goes through it. Early jets were pure turbojets (BPR 0) — all the air went through the hot core and left as a screaming, wasteful jet. Modern airliner engines send the large majority of their air around the core.

  • BPR ~0–1 — turbojets and low-bypass (fast, loud, thirsty; now mostly military).
  • BPR ~5–12.5 — modern high-bypass commercial engines. The Pratt & Whitney PW1000G geared turbofan reaches about 12.5:1.
  • effective BPR >30 — open (unducted) fans, the frontier.

The geared fan trick

There's a conflict: a big fan wants to turn slowly (its blade tips go supersonic and lose efficiency otherwise), but the low-pressure turbine driving it wants to turn fast (it's small and makes more power per stage at speed). For decades they were bolted to the same shaft, forcing a compromise. The geared turbofan puts a planetary reduction gearbox between them, so the fan turns slowly and the turbine turns fast — each at its own optimum. That decoupling is what unlocked bypass ratios past 12:1.

03 Thermal efficiency: hotter and higher-pressure

The core is a Brayton cycle, and its efficiency climbs with two things, pushed together:

Overall pressure ratio (OPR)

How much the air is compressed from inlet to combustor. More compression extracts more work from each kilo of fuel. Older engines sat around OPR 30; modern large engines like the GE9X reach roughly OPR 60:1. The limit is that compressed air gets hot just from being squeezed, which eats into the headroom you have before the turbine.

Turbine inlet temperature (TIT)

The hotter the gas entering the turbine, the more energy you can extract. Modern cores run turbine inlet temperatures above 1,500°C — and here's the remarkable part: that is hotter than the melting point of the metal the blades are made from. The blades survive only because of how they're cooled and coated (next section). Raising OPR and TIT together is the main thermal-efficiency lever.

Why "together"? Raising temperature without raising pressure, or vice-versa, quickly hits diminishing returns and can even hurt. The gains come from moving both up the cycle in step — which is exactly why thermal-efficiency progress is slow and materials-limited.

04 Materials & cooling: surviving the core

If you want a hotter core, you need parts that tolerate it. This is where a huge amount of real efficiency hides:

  • Single-crystal superalloy blades — turbine blades grown as one crystal, with no grain boundaries to creep or crack under load and heat.
  • Film cooling — cool air bled from the compressor is routed through tiny holes in the blades, forming a protective film over the surface. Clever, but every kilo of air used for cooling is a kilo not making thrust.
  • Thermal barrier coatings — ceramic coatings that insulate the metal from the gas.
  • Ceramic matrix composites (CMCs) — the big recent step. CMCs tolerate high heat with far less cooling air, freeing that air to do useful work. The GE9X uses CMCs in the hot section. Less cooling bleed is a direct efficiency gain.

05 Weight & aerodynamics

Efficiency isn't only thermodynamics — a lighter engine burns less fuel carrying itself around.

  • Composite fan blades & case — the fan is the largest-diameter part of the engine, so weight saved there counts double. Carbon-fibre blades and casings cut substantial mass.
  • 3D aerodynamic blading — computational design of blade shapes that squeeze a little more efficiency from every compressor and turbine stage.
  • Tighter tip clearances & sealing — air that leaks around a blade tip does no work. Minimising those gaps, and better seals, recovers fractions of a percent that add up.

06 The frontier: the open fan

The nacelle around a ducted fan adds weight and drag, and it physically caps how big the fan can be. The boldest concept — such as CFM's RISE program — removes the duct entirely: a single large open (unducted) rotor at the front, followed by a stage of variable-pitch stator vanes. That pushes the effective bypass ratio far beyond anything a ducted fan can reach.

The stated goal is around a 20% reduction in fuel burn and CO₂ versus today's engines, designed to run on up to 100% sustainable aviation fuel (SAF) and compatible with hydrogen, with a target entry into service in the mid-2030s. The hard parts are noise and installing a huge unducted rotor on an airframe — real engineering problems, not solved yet.
